## Description

## Finding (the SO-81 audit, Nov 12)

A Timer becomes ready when its interrupt is delivered and serviced. The
kernel then counts every period it missed (`fire` in `kernel/core/time.saw`),
so the count is right as of that service. Between services it lags the clock.

Under heavy host load the audit saw a process spin through about five timer
periods of its own clock while the kernel took one timer interrupt. A wait at
that moment reported one fire. On an emulator that is the host starving the
emulator's timer thread; on hardware the same lag is any latency between an
expiry and its interrupt being taken, which is small but not zero.

## The question

Should a wait that looks at a Timer first expire it against the clock, so
that the count it reports is as of the wait and never lags, whatever the
interrupt's latency?

- **For:** "several periods have passed, so the count is above one" becomes
  true by construction for any caller that can read the clock, on a loaded
  emulator and on hardware alike.
- **Against:** a wait would do work that today runs only in interrupt
  context, and readiness would no longer mean "the interrupt was delivered
  and serviced", which spec §2.2 and §2.6 are written around.

The tests no longer depend on the answer: SO-81 made them wait on the
kernel's own interrupt count.

**Brief (lead): a wait expires the Timers it looks at against the clock.** The user ruled yes on Nov 14.

**What is built.**
- **Kernel.** When a wait (and anything else that reads a Timer's readiness or its count for a process: find each reader) looks at an armed Timer, it first brings the Timer up to the clock: if the expiry has passed, the Timer is ready and its count includes every period that has passed as of that look, by the arithmetic `fire` already runs when the interrupt is serviced (`kernel/core/time.saw`). The clock is read once per wait, not once per Timer. The interrupt path is unchanged in what it does, and the two must agree: a period is counted once, whichever of them saw it first, and a Timer the wait brought up to date does not fire again for the same period when its interrupt arrives.
- **Spec.** §2.2 and §2.6 are written around "ready when the interrupt was delivered and serviced". They change to: a Timer is ready once its expiry has passed, as of its interrupt or of a wait that looked, and the count a wait reports is as of the wait. State what a one-shot and a periodic Timer report, what re-arming and disarming do to a Timer the wait has brought up to date, and that nothing else about waits changes.
- **Interrupts stay masked in the kernel**, so the look and the interrupt path cannot interleave on one processor; say so where the code relies on it, and keep the look bounded (no preemption point is added).

**Cases.** No case asserts a value that depends on host time (CLAUDE.md has the rule and how to build a case that needs an event to have happened).
- The property the ruling is for, held by construction and not by the clock's speed: a process arms a periodic Timer, reads the clock until several periods have certainly passed (it can read the clock; a stalled host only makes more pass), then waits: the count is at least that many, on every profile, whether or not the kernel took an interrupt in between. Before the change the case must fail when no interrupt was serviced in that span; find a way to make that so by construction (the existing cases that mask or hold off the tick show how), and show the case red on main.
- A one-shot whose expiry has passed is ready at the wait without its interrupt having been serviced, and its interrupt arriving later does not make it ready a second time.
- A Timer brought up to date by a wait and then disarmed, and one re-armed, report what the spec says.
- Each new line is shown to hold by a one-operator mutant (the comparison with the expiry, the period count's arithmetic, the "counted once" guard), red then green.

**Scope.** `kernel/core/time.saw`, `waitables.saw` and whatever reads a Timer for a wait; `spec.md`; the cases. Another unit is changing `kernel/core/process.saw`, `dispatch.saw`'s slab code, `limits.saw` and the boot path: stay out of those files.

**Lead: SO-83.p1 is up: a wait expires its Timers against the clock.** Gate on the implementer's machine: 792 / 6 / 39 (main's 789 plus the new case on three profiles); no existing case or expectation moved.

**What it does.**
- `time.saw`: `catch_up_timer(slot, now)` runs `TimerSlot.fire(now)` for a live Timer that is due, without the wake: every period passed is counted by the interrupt's own division, a periodic Timer's deadline moves past `now`, a one-shot disarms. So whichever of the look and the interrupt runs second finds nothing due.
- `waitables.saw`: `waiter_ready_attachment`, the scan behind `Waiter.Wait` and the wait leg of `ReplyWait`, brings each Timer attachment it reaches up to the clock before asking whether it is ready. The clock is read once, at the first Timer the scan reaches. A Timer the scan brings up is ready, so the scan returns it and the same call delivers it.
- The hardware comparator is not reprogrammed after a look: the old deadline's interrupt arrives, finds nothing due and reprograms as it does today.
- `spec.md` §2.6 and §2.2 say it: ready once the expiry has passed, as of the interrupt or of a wait that looked; the count is as of the wait; a period is counted once; arming or disarming clears a count a wait brought up and had not delivered.

**The case, and why it is in the kernel.** A process cannot hold off its own timer interrupt, so "the deadline passed and no interrupt was serviced" cannot be made true by construction from a process: there the change shows only under delivery latency. `timer_asof` is a harness kernel with the tick never started, so no interrupt is taken, and it reads the Timer's own count before the look (`untouched=1`). It reaches the wait path through a new module, `kernel/core/probe.saw`, which builds one Timer attached to one Waiter and calls the real `waiter_ready_attachment`, `waitable_answer` and `waitable_consume`, and runs `on_timer_interrupt` by hand (made `public(package)` for that). Nothing in a shipping kernel imports it and images link with `--gc-sections`. **For the reviewer to judge:** whether a probe module in `kcore` is the right seam for this, and that it leaves the gate's images byte for byte (the implementer reports no existing row moved).

Every number is bracketed by clock reads the case takes itself (`owed(first, before) <= count <= owed(first, after)`), so nothing asserts a duration and a stalled host moves both ends together. Red on unchanged main, riscv32: `atleast=0 bracketed=0`, `oneshot fires=0 disarmed=0 second=1`, `up=0` twice.

**Mutants, each alone, riscv32:** `is_due` negated; `is_due` replaced by `armed` (panics on the far re-arm); `fire`'s `base + step` to `base` (both counted-once lines 0); `fire`'s `+ 1` to `+ 2` (the second look's counted-once is 0; the first look's upper bracket is loose on a slow host and does not see it); the clock never read; the call removed (the red-on-main run).

**Left as it is, said plainly.**
- `waiter_add`'s readiness check at attach is unchanged: it only decides whether to wake a parked thread and reports no count, and an expired Timer's interrupt is already pending.
- "The clock is read once per wait" is not held by a case (one Timer per Waiter in the probe); reading it per Timer would differ only in which reading a later Timer is judged against.
- No process-level case was added: through the syscall path the property holds on main too whenever the interrupt was serviced, so such a case could not be shown red.

Requested changes on patch SO-83.p1 (revision 1)

**Changes requested (reviewer, Nov 14), revision 1: one more path hands a process a Timer's count, and it does not look at the clock.** The wait path is right and its case holds. The attach path still reports the count as of the last interrupt serviced, which the note says cannot happen and the new spec sentence rules out.

**The finding: a wait answered by an attach.**

- `waiter_add` (`kernel/core/dispatch.saw`) ends with `if waitable_ready(target.kind, target.slot) { wake_one_waiter(w, a) }`. `wake_one_waiter` calls `deliver_attachment`, which writes `waitable_answer`'s payload, `TIMERS[slot].fires`, into the parked thread's record. So a thread parked on a Waiter gets a Timer's count from the attach, with no look.
- The note says this check "only decides whether to wake a parked thread and reports no count". It does report one, through that wake.
- The spec sentence this patch adds, "So the count a wait reports is as of the wait and does not lag the Timer's interrupt", is false of that wait. The brief asked for it: "anything else that reads a Timer's readiness or its count for a process: find each reader".

**The probe** (chat file f93; real syscalls, two threads, on the patch). A Timer with an interval of 1 ns, so the counts delivered say what instant they are as of. Each round: the worker sees the kernel's interrupt count move, reads the clock, attaches the Timer to a Waiter the other thread is parked on, and reads the interrupt count again. A round is *stale* when the count is as of an instant before that clock reading, and *certified* when the interrupt count did not move between the detection and after the attach.

| profile | run 1: certified and stale | run 2 | furthest behind |
|---|---|---|---|
| riscv32 | 40 of 40 | 40 of 40 | 268 µs |
| arm64 | 40 of 40 | 35 of 40 | 20 µs |
| riscv32-flat | 40 of 40 | 40 of 40 | 26 µs |

One riscv32 round as printed: `as-of 220395001..220544001 attached-after 220775000 interrupts 102 102`. The base revision gives the same.

With a look before that check (two edits: `catch_up_timer` joins the `kcore.time` import, and a `match target.kind { case Timer -> { catch_up_timer(target.slot, hal.timer_now_ns()) }, case _ -> {} }` precedes the `if`), no round is stale on any profile, two runs each, and all 40 are as of the reading or later on riscv32.

**What I ask for, one of two.** Which is the ruling's is not mine to say; the issue's question was a count that "never lags".

1. The look at the attach's check, with a case. The probe's attach half is one: with the look, "not stale" holds by construction (the count is as of the attach, which is after the reading).
2. Or the spec says what a wait answered by an attach reports (the count the Timer held, as of the last interrupt serviced), and the note's reason is corrected.

**The readers, as I found them.** A Timer's count reaches a process only through `deliver_attachment`. Its callers are the two waits, after the scan, and `wake_one_waiter`, which is reached from the interrupt (`fire_timer`), an invalidation (`invalidate_timer`, count cleared first) and the attach. So the attach is the one left.

**What holds.**

- **The wait path.** `waiter_ready_attachment` has two callers besides the probe module, `waiter_wait` and `ReplyWait`'s wait leg. A Timer the look brings up is ready, so it is the attachment the scan returns, and a Timer's delivery has no refusal path (by reading `deliver_attachment`), so nothing is brought up and left undelivered. Several threads on one Waiter (by reading too): a look by one thread takes the fire a parked one would have had, which is one fire to one thread, as on main.
- **Through the real syscall.** The probe's wait half on riscv32: on the base revision 40 of 40 rounds are stale in both runs; on the patch none is, and all 40 are as of the reading or later. On arm64 and riscv32-flat the probe cannot tell either way on either revision (the lag there is smaller than its bracket around the arm).
- **The case.** `timer_asof` passed 12 of 12 on each profile with eight cores kept busy, and 60 more on riscv32. Two mutants of mine, each alone: the look given `0` for the clock, and `is_due` negated in `catch_up_timer`. Both are red on riscv32, arm64 and riscv32-flat.
- **The probe module, which the note asks me to judge.** The shipping kernel's IR has no `timer_probe` name (the harness kernel's has them). `kernel-main.o` and `timer_interval.elf` are byte for byte the same with `probe.saw` in the tree and with it moved out. As a seam it is sound: it calls the real scan, answer and consume, never a copy.

**Not blocking.**

- **A process-level case can be shown red on main.** The note says one could not. The probe's wait half is one on riscv32: a 1 ns interval, the first deadline 20 ms out so the two readings around the arm are a call apart, a wait for the kernel's interrupt count to move, a clock reading, then the wait. "Not stale" holds by construction on the patch and failed 40 of 40 on the base revision. It is not red on the other two profiles, so it adds to the harness case and does not replace it.
- **One run I cannot explain.** `timer_interval` printed `late=0` once in 112 runs on riscv32 with eight cores busy, on the patch; none in 40 on the base revision. `late=0` means the count was one, a clock reading equal to the deadline. I do not attribute it to the patch: I could not reproduce it or find a likely route. A wait's look is a second place where a reading can equal a deadline, and that case's header reasons only about the interrupt ("which no interrupt can be on time for").

**Lead: SO-83.p1 revision 2. The attach looks at the clock, a look programs the hardware, and two cases are added.** The lead made this revision. The reviewer's finding is right, and the note's sentence that the attach "reports no count" was the implementer's claim, passed on by the lead unchecked.

**1. The attach (the finding; the first of the reviewer's two choices).** `waiter_add` brings a Timer up to the clock before its level-triggered check, so the record that check hands a parked thread carries a count as of the attach. `spec.md` §2.6 says so, and names the three ways a count reaches a process: a wait that finds the Timer ready (as of the wait), an attach that answers a parked wait (as of the attach), and the interrupt waking a parked thread (as of its own reading).

**2. A look that counts a fire programs the hardware again (found in this revision's repeats).** Revision 1 left the comparator on the deadline a look had already counted, so an interrupt arrived that found nothing due. That breaks the reasoning the Timer cases rest on, "the kernel took two interrupts, so two fires are pending": in ten runs of `timer_interval` on revision 1 plus the attach look, riscv32-flat printed `coalesced=0` once (`tick two fires=2`, then the spin's two interrupts were the stale one and one real fire). `catch_up_timer` now calls `reprogram_timer` after a fire, as an interrupt's service does. This is not a repeat-count argument: the harness case holds it by construction (below).

**3. Cases.**
- **`timer_asof_calls`** (new, `tests/timer-asof`, a process; the reviewer's probe reduced to an assertion). A Timer at a one-microsecond interval, so the fires delivered, summed, say what instant they are as of. Each round waits for the kernel to take an interrupt, reads the clock a few times, keeps the last reading `c0`, and then takes the count: by a wait (first part), by attaching the Timer to a Waiter the other thread is parked on (second part). The kernel's look is inside that call, after `c0`, so `total * interval + ca >= c0`, where `ca` bounds the first deadline from a reading after the arm. `stale=0` is asserted for both parts. A stall between `c0` and the call only raises the count; one around the arm only raises `ca`. The interval is a microsecond and not a nanosecond so that a machine word of fires cannot saturate inside the harness's timeout (at 1 ns a 32-bit count saturates in about four seconds, and a stalled host could then read as stale). The tick is off, so the two threads change places only where one parks.
- **`timer_asof`** (the harness case) gains `due=1 quiet=1` on its one-shot line: the case waits until the timer's interrupt is pending, and after the look nothing is scheduled, so the hardware has been programmed to never and the interrupt is no longer pending, however long the host takes. The probe's arm and disarm now program the hardware as the ops do.
- **`timer_interval`'s `late=1`** is now by construction: after arming at a one-nanosecond interval the program reads the clock until a reading is past the latest instant the first deadline can be, then waits; the count is as of the wait, so it is the first deadline and at least one period more. Before, the claim rested on no interrupt being serviced at the deadline's own clock reading, which a coarse emulator clock does not promise; that is one route to the `late=0` the reviewer saw once and could not place, on either revision. The lead did not reproduce it.

**4. Mutants, each alone, all three profiles unless said.**

| Mutant | Result |
|---|---|
| the look at the attach removed | `timer_asof_calls` red on riscv32, arm64 and riscv32-flat: `attach stale` above zero on each (furthest behind 830, 73 and 79 µs) |
| the look in the wait's scan removed | `timer_asof_calls` red on all three: `wait stale=3`, `1`, `1` of 40. This half is thin on arm64 and flat, as the reviewer found; the harness case is what holds the wait path there |
| `reprogram_timer()` removed from `catch_up_timer` | `timer_asof` red on all three: `due=1 quiet=0`. Forty runs of `timer_interval` with this mutant did not go red, which is why the harness leg exists |

Each source file was restored byte for byte (`cmp`).

**5. Runs.** The three cases thirty times on every profile after the attach look and the reprogram, and fifteen more on the final tree: no failure. Every case whose name has timer, waiter, wait, sleep, interval, preempt or reply in it, once: 123 passed across the three profiles. `make sos-smoke-esp32c3 sos-smoke-esp32s3`: 6 and 39.
